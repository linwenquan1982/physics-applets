📘 Physics Guide — H2 Lesson

A charged particle enters a uniform magnetic field of flux density $B$ at an angle $\theta$ to the field. The motion that follows is one of the most elegant results in H2 Physics. Let's break it down step-by-step.
1 The Scenario

A particle of charge $q$ and mass $m$ enters a uniform magnetic field of flux density $B$ (pointing in the $+x$ direction) with speed $v$ at an angle $\theta$ to the field.

The magnitude of the magnetic force on a moving charged particle is given by:

$F = Bqv\sin\theta$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the velocity and the magnetic field. The direction of $F$ is found using Fleming's Left-Hand Rule for a positive charge (reverse it for a negative charge).

💡 Key idea
The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work on the charge — the speed $v$ stays constant; only the direction changes.
2 Resolve the Velocity

The trick to handling 3D motion is to resolve the velocity into two perpendicular components:

$v_\parallel = v\cos\theta \quad\text{(along } B\text{)}$
$v_\perp = v\sin\theta \quad\text{(perpendicular to } B\text{)}$

We then analyse each component independently, then superpose the two motions to recover the full 3D trajectory.

  • v∥ — the component parallel to $B$
  • v⊥ — the component perpendicular to $B$
3 View Along $B$ (Side X)

Look along the magnetic field. From this angle, you only see v∥, which points in the same direction as $B$.

The angle between $v_\parallel$ and $B$ is $0°$, so:

$F_\parallel = Bqv_\parallel \sin(0°) = 0$

No force ⇒ no acceleration ⇒ constant velocity.

The particle drifts along the field direction in a perfectly straight line at speed $v_\parallel = v\cos\theta$.

4 View Perpendicular to $B$ (Side Y)

Now look at the particle from the side — so $B$ goes into the page (×). Only the component v⊥ matters here, and it is perpendicular to $B$ ($\theta = 90°$).

By Fleming's Left-Hand Rule, the magnetic force has magnitude:

$F_B = Bqv_\perp$

This force is always perpendicular to v⊥. It cannot change the particle's speed, only bend its direction. This is the exact condition for uniform circular motion.

Setting $F_B$ equal to the centripetal force $\dfrac{mv_\perp^2}{r}$:

$Bqv_\perp = \dfrac{mv_\perp^2}{r} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; r = \dfrac{mv_\perp}{|q|B}$

The period of one full orbit is:

$T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v_\perp} = \dfrac{2\pi m}{|q|B}$
💡 Striking result
$T$ depends only on $m$, $|q|$ and $B$ — not on $v_\perp$! Faster particles trace bigger circles, but each orbit still takes the same time.
5 Combine → Helical Motion

Now superpose the two motions:

  • v∥ → straight-line drift along $B$
  • v⊥ → circular motion in the plane perpendicular to $B$

The result is a helix — a 3D spiral, like a stretched spring wound along $B$.

The pitch (distance the particle advances along $B$ during one full circular orbit):

$p = v_\parallel \, T = \dfrac{2\pi m \, v\cos\theta}{|q|B}$

★ Real-World Connection

Helical motion of charged particles in magnetic fields explains:

  • Auroras — solar wind protons and electrons spiral along Earth's field lines toward the poles.
  • Mass spectrometers — measure $m/q$ from the radius $r = mv/(qB)$.
  • Cyclotrons — exploit the $v$-independent period $T$ to keep accelerating particles in resonance.
  • Magnetic confinement fusion (tokamaks) — keep hot plasma spiralling along field lines, away from the walls.
